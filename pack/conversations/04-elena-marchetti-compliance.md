# Conversation 4 — Elena Marchetti, Head of Compliance

Sits outside the desk but sets rules it is supposed to follow. Fictional transcript, lightly edited.

---

**You have seen the plan to speed the desk up. What is your reaction?**

My reaction is that speed is the wrong objective for about one message in fifteen, and those are the ones I care about.

Most of what arrives there should be faster, I agree. Gate passes, delivery slots, forms. Fine. But there is a small category where the current slowness is the only control we have, and it is accidental — it exists because nobody has got round to it, not because anyone designed it. If you remove the friction without replacing it, you have removed a control you did not know you had.

**Which categories?**

Four. Anything granting access to a data room on a live transaction. Anything changing bank details or payment instructions. Anything adding a new counterparty or vendor to our systems. And anything signed on our letterhead that commits us to a third party.

**Take the data room one.**

The rule is that access is not granted until the NDA is executed and back with us. What happens in practice is that a law firm writes in, says the NDA is with your side, asks for access in parallel because of timing pressure, and somebody helpful on the desk opens it.

That is not a small thing. That is confidential information about a live transaction going to a party we have no enforceable agreement with. The pressure is always genuine and always urgent, which is exactly why the answer has to be structural rather than a matter of who happens to pick it up.

**And bank details?**

There is a written rule that a change of bank details received by email is verified by telephone, on a number we already held — not one from the email. I would be astonished if that happened reliably. The people on the desk are not trained to be suspicious, and the fraudulent versions are convincing. They arrive right after a genuine invoice chase, which is not a coincidence. Someone is reading something.

**So what do you want a product to do?**

For those four categories, I want the fast path closed. Not a warning banner — those get clicked through in a fortnight. I want the action to be genuinely unavailable until the prerequisite exists: no data room access until an executed NDA is recorded against that counterparty, no payment detail change until a verification has been logged with a name and a date on it.

**Omar's position is that anything which adds steps kills adoption.**

Omar is describing gate passes and I am describing four categories. If the product cannot tell the difference between them then yes, we have a real conflict and someone has to decide. If it can, we do not.

Although I would add — I do not want the machine deciding which category something is in and then hard-blocking on that basis. If it wrongly classifies a routine message as a bank detail change, it has now made an ordinary task impossible, and that is how the control gets disabled by whoever is senior enough to disable it. The classification can be the machine's. The consequence of being wrong should be recoverable.

**What would you accept as evidence it is working?**

A record I can inspect afterwards. Who released what, on what basis, and what existed at the time. At the moment, if I asked how a particular data room access was granted in January, the honest answer is that we would have to reconstruct it from memory.
